First performance: June 19, 1988, at the Alpine Valley Music Theatre in East Troy, Wisconsin. "Foolish Heart" opened the second set, and was followed by "Playing in the Band." The song remained in the repertoire thereafter, most often appearing in the second set, following "Victim or the Crime."

Search for where the rivers end, or where the rivers start

Over 100 years ago, the world was still filled with wonders and unknown places waiting to be discovered. A popular undertaking of the time was the search for the source of the Nile in Africa, the mother river. One of the most famous efforts at finding 'where the rivers start' was the one made by Dr. David Livingstone, who is credited with the opening up and the settling of the vast, uncharted African territory. He never found the source of the Nile, but he did get to stagger up to Henry Stanley, who then got to say the infamous punch line: "Dr. Livingstone, I presume?"

Speak with wisdom like a child

This reminds me of the expression "out of the mouths of babes", an expression meaning that sometimes innocence can give birth to great wisdom (or if you prefer, "kids say the darndest things"). This expression comes from the Bible, Psalms 8:2--
"Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings hast thou ordained strength because of thine enemies, that thou mightest still the enemy and the avenger."
You can find this expression in all sorts of places, particularly as a genre of jokes or humerous anectdotes, where the expression has come to mean inadvertant truth or humor.

And, most cosmically, in Star Trek: The Original Series, the epidode entitled A PIECE OF THE ACTION contains the following dialog:

Kirk: "Out of the mouths of babes..."
Young Man: "Who you callin' a babe?"
Kirk: "I'm calling you a babe... but don't take it personally."

Crown yourself the king of clowns

A famous clown named Lou Jacobs billed himself "The King of Clowns". Jacobs was born in 1903 and died in 1992. He joined Ringling & Barnum & Bailey Circus in 1925. There he created one of the most famous clown gags ever...the midget car.

Or stand way back apart

Compare Sonnet XIX (On his Blindness) by John Milton. The last line is "They also serve who only stand and wait.''

Never look around the bend

Recalls the Credence Clearwater Revival classic song "Up Around The Bend."

Also, the expression 'going around the bend' is idiomatic for going crazy.

Bite that hand

The saying "bite the hand that feeds them" first appeared in Edmund Burke's Thoughts and Details on Scarcity (1800):
"And having looked to Government for bread, on the very first scarcity they will turn and bite the hand that fed them."

Dare to leap

Compare the lines in "Row Jimmy":
"Here's a half a dollar if you dare
Double twist when you hit the air"

Where angels fear to tread

This refers to a line by Alexander Pope, in his poem "An Essay on Criticism" (1711), in which Pope takes critics, with their easy slander, to task:
"The bookful blockhead, ignorantly read,
With loads of learned lumber in his head,
With his own tongue still edifies his ears,
And always list'ning to himself appears.
...
No place so sacred from such fops is barred,
Nor is Paul's church more safe than Paul's churchyard:
Nay, fly to Altars ; there they'll talk you dead :
For Fools rush in where Angels fear to tread."
